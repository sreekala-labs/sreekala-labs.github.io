Greatest Common Divisor(GCD) of two integers is the largest positive integer that divides both numbers without leaving a remainder.

For example: 

GCD of 4 and 6 is 2. 

GCD of 7 and 13 is 1 

GCD of 100 and 200 is 100. 

**In-built Python:**

math library in Python provides the method to calculate GCD of numbers. 

```python
import math

# For two numbers
print(math.gcd(60, 48))  # Output: 12

# For multiple numbers (Python 3.9+)
print(math.gcd(12, 24, 36, 60))  # Output: 12
```

**Naive Solution:** 

```python
def GCD(a,b):
    #get the minimum of a and b
    res= min(a,b)
    while res > 0:
        #Loop until both have no reminders.
        if a%res ==0 and b%res==0:
            break
        res-=1 
    return res
```

**Optimal Solution:**

```python
def GCD(a,b):
    if b==0: return a 
    return GCD(b, a%b)
```

The above code is based on the concept of Eucledian Algorithm. 

Eucledian algorithm is based on a simple mathematical principle: *the GCD of two numbers also divides their difference.*

By extension, instead of repeatedly subtracting the smaller number from the larger number, 
we can use the remainder of their division (the modulo operation). This drastically speeds up the process.

Let's find the {gcd}(252, 105) manually to see how it works step-by-step:

| Step | Larger | Smaller| Operation (a div b)| Remainder (a mod b) |
| --- | --- | ---|---|---|
| 1 |252| 105| 252 = (2 * 105) + 42| 42| 
| 2| 105| 42| 105 = (2 * 42) + 21 | 21|
| 3| 42| 21|  42 = (2 * 21) + 0 | 0 | 

Time Complexity: O(log(min(a, b)))

Explanation: In the worst-case scenario, the numbers shrink at a geometric rate. Even if you input incredibly large numbers 
(e.g., hundreds of digits long), the algorithm will find the answer in a matter of seconds.

The "Worst Case": The absolute slowest inputs for the Euclidean algorithm are consecutive Fibonacci numbers 
(like 34 and 21), because their remainders always yield the next lowest Fibonacci number, maximizing the number of steps required.
